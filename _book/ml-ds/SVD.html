11  Singular Value Decomposition

11.1 Singular Vectors

Definition 11.1 (First right singular vector) The first right singular vector \(v_1\) of \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) is defined as: \(v_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d\), and \(v_1 = \text{argmax}_{\|v\|_2=1} \|Xv\|_2\) and the first singular value is \(\sigma_1 = \|Xv_1\|_2\)

Proposition 11.1 Definition 11.1 is equivalent as \[ v_1 = \underset{\|v\|_2=1}{\mathrm{argmin}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \|x_j - P_{v_1}(x_j)\|_2^2, \] where \(P_{v_1}(x_j)\) is the projected vector of \(x_j\) in the subspace spanned by \(v_1\). Here, we have \[ X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} = \begin{pmatrix} -x_1^T- \\ -x_2^T- \\ \vdots \\ -x_n^T- \end{pmatrix} \]

Proof. \[ \begin{align} \sum_{j} \|x_j - P_{v}(x_j)\|_2^2 &= \sum_{ij}\left(x_{i,j}-\langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_j\rangle v_i\right)^2 \\ &=\sum_{ij}x^2_{i, j} + \langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_j\rangle v_i^2 - 2x_{i,j}v_i\langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_j \rangle\\ &=\sum_{ij}x^2_{i,j} + \sum_j\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_j\rangle ^2 -2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_j\rangle^2\right)\\ &=C - \sum_j\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_j\rangle^2=C-\|X\mathbf{v}\|^2_2 \end{align} \] where \(C\) is a constant. Thus we have the \[ \underset{\|v\|_2=1}{\mathrm{argmin}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \|x_j - P_{v_1}(x_j)\|_2^2 = \underset{\|v\|_2=1}{\mathrm{argmax}} \|Xv\|_2^2 \]

Note

Proposition 11.1 shows that the first singular vector can be understood as a line that minimizes least square error of projected data, i.e., a best fitting line.

Next, we continue to define the second singular vector.

Definition 11.2 (Second Singular Vector) The second right singular vector \(v_2 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is defined as \[ v_2 = \underset{{v \perp v_1, \|v\|_2=1}}{\mathrm{argmin}} \|Xv\|_2, \] and second singular value defined correspondingly as \[ \sigma_2 = \|Xv_2\|_2 \]

Proposition 11.2 (Best-Fitting 2D Subspace) Let \(v_1, v_2\) be the first and second right singular vectors of \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\). Let \(V = \text{span}(v_1, v_2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) be the two-dimensional subspace they span.

Then \(V\) is the solution to the following minimization: \[ V = \underset{W:\,\dim(W)=2}{\mathrm{argmin}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \|x_j - P_W(x_j)\|_2^2 \] where \(P_W:\mathbb{R}^d\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2\) is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace \(W\).

Proof. Let \(w_1, w_2\) be two orthonormal bases for any 2-dimensional subspace \(W\). We can always select \(w_2\) s.t. \(\langle w_2,v_1 \rangle = 0\) (such \(w_2\) can be found by the intersection between \(W\) and subspace that perpendicular to \(v_1\)).

A figure perhaps showing the constructiong of w1 and w2 \[ \begin{align} &\sum_j \|x_j - \langle P_W(x_j)\rangle\|_2^2 = \sum_j \langle x_j-P_W(x_j), x_j-P_W(x_j) \rangle\\ ={}&\sum_j\langle x_j - P_w(x_j), x_j\rangle + \sum_j\langle x_j - P_W(x_j), -P_W(x_j)\rangle\\ ={}&\sum_j\|x_j\|^2_2 - 2\sum_j\langle P_W(x_j), x_j\rangle + \|P_W(x_j)\|^2_2\\ \end{align}. \] Noticed that \[ \begin{align} \langle P_W(x_j), x_j \rangle &= \langle w_1\langle w_1, x_j\rangle + w_2\langle w_2, x_j\rangle, x_j\rangle\\ & = \langle w_1, x_j\rangle^2 + \langle w_2, x_j\rangle^2=\|P_W(x_j)\|^2_2, \end{align} \] thus \[ \sum_j\|x_j - P_W(x_j)\|^2_2 = C - (\sum_j \langle w_1, x_j \rangle^2 + \langle w_2, x_j \rangle^2), \] where \[ C = \sum_{j=1}^n \| x_j \|_2^2 \] is a constant.

Since \(v_1\) maximizes \(\|Xv\|_2^2\) among all \(\|v\|_2=1\) , and \(v_2\) maximizes \(\|Xv\|_2^2\) among \(v \perp v_1\) and \(\|v\|_2=1, thus\)$ _jw_1,x_jsum_jv_1, x_j, _jw_2, x_jv_2, x_j $$ and thus \(V\) minimize \(\|x_j-P_W(x_j)\|^2_2\) among all 2-dim subspace.

Note

Definition 11.3 (r-right Singular Vector) The leading \(r\) right singular vectors \(v_1, \dots, v_r \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are defined as:

\[ v_k := \underset{ \substack{ \|v\|_2=1,\, \\ v \perp \text{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}\} } }{\mathrm{argmax}}{} \|Xv\|_2 \]

with the corresponding singular values as \(\sigma_k = \|Xv_k\|_2\).

Proposition 11.3 (Greedy construction for best-fit subspace) Let \(v_1, \dots, v_r\) be the leading right singular vectors constructed by Definition 11.3, and \(V = \mathrm{span}(v_1, \dots, v_r) \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) be the subspace spanned by them. Then \[ V \in \underset{ \substack{W\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d \\ \mathrm{dim}(W) = r \\ \text{linear subspace}} }{\mathrm{argmin}}\sum_j \|x_j - P_W(x_j)\|_2^2 \] i.e., \(V\) is the best-fit linear subspace in terms of least square error.

Remark 11.1.

  • Proposition 11.3 can be proved similarly as in the 2 dimensional case (Proposition 11.2).
  • Proposition 11.3 shows that the greedy construction of r-right singularvectors formed a best-fit subspace in terms of least square error.

11.2 Singular Value Decomposition

Theorem 11.1 (Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)) For any matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), there exist orthogonal matrices \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) and \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) and a non-negative diagonal matrix \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) such that

\[ X_{n \times d} = U_{n \times n}\, \Sigma_{n \times d}\, V_{d \times d}^T \]

Remark 11.2.

  • The diagonals of \(\Sigma\), arranged in non-increasing order, are the singular values defined in Proposition 11.3.
  • Columns of \(U\) are the left singular vectors of \(X\).
  • The left singular vectors \(u_i := \frac{1}{\sigma_i} X v_i\), where \(\sigma_i\) is the \(i\)-th diagonal element of \(\Sigma\).
  • Columns of \(V\) are the right singular vectors of \(X\) defined in Proposition 11.3.
  • SVD can be viewed as ‘generalized diagonolization’ of an arbitrary matrix.

The existence of SVD can be shown as follow

Proof 11.1. Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) and \(C = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), then \(C\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Let the orthogonal decomposition of \(C\) be: \[ C = V \Lambda V^T, \] where \(V^T V = I\) and \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_d), \lambda_i \geq 0 ,\forall i\in\{1, 2, \cdots, d\}\).

Consider \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) such that \(\Sigma^T \Sigma = \Lambda\). If \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = r \leq d\), we can arrange the diagonal element of \(\Lambda\) s.t. \(\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_r\geq0,\lambda_{r+1} = \cdots=\lambda_d=0\). Then \[ \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\cdots,\sqrt{\lambda_r}) & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

We define the left singular vector \(u_i\) as: \[ u_i := \frac{1}{\sigma_i} X v_i,\quad i = 1, \dots, r. \] Then \(u_i\) are orthonormal: \[ u_i^T u_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T X^T X v_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T C v_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \lambda_j \delta_{ij} = \delta_{ij}. \] We can then extend \(u_{r+1}, \dots, u_n\) such that \(U = [u_1\,|\,u_2\,|\,\dots\,|\,u_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is orthogonal.
Thus, we have \(\sigma_i u_i = X v_i\) for \(i=1,\ldots, r\) and \(X v_i = 0\) for \(i > r\), which gives \(X = U \Sigma V^T\).

Remark 11.3. This proof can be extended to \(X \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times d}\) with unitary matrices \(U\) and \(V\).

11.3 Greedy Construction and SVD

Proposition 11.4 The SVD of \(X = U \Sigma V^T\) is the same as the greedy construction; i.e., \(V\) and \(\Sigma\) are right singular vectors and singular values constructed by the greedy definitions.

Proof 11.2. Let \(v_1' := \mathrm{argmax}_{\|v\|_2=1} \|Xv\|_2\) be the first right singular vector.
Write \(X = U \Sigma V^T\) where \(V = [v_1, \dots, v_d] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) and \(v_i^T v_j = \delta_{ij}\).
Then: \[ \|Xv\|_2 = \|U \Sigma V^T v\|_2 = \|\Sigma V^T v\|_2 \] Let \(z = V^T v\), then \(\|z\|_2 = 1\) since \(V\) is orthogonal and \(\|v\|_2 = 1\).
So \[ \|Xv\|_2 = \|\Sigma z\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i} (\sigma_i z_i)^2} \] Suppose \(\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r\). Notice \[ \sum \sigma_i^2 z_i^2 \leq \sigma_1^2 \left(\sum z_i^2\right) = \sigma_1^2 \] Equality is attained when \(z_1 = 1\) \(\Rightarrow v = v_1 \Rightarrow v_1' = v_1\).

Next, \(v_2' := \mathrm{argmax}_{\|v\|_2=1,\, v \perp v_1} \|Xv\|_2\). We proceed similarly, but now \(z_1 = 0\) (since \(v \perp v_1\)).
Then \[ \|Xv\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=2} (\sigma_i z_i)^2} \leq \sigma_2 \] Equality when \(z_2 = 1 \Rightarrow v_2' = v_2\).
Similarly, we find all \(v_i\) by the greedy construction.

11.4 Skinny SVD and Full SVD

Full SVD:
\[ X_{n \times d} = U_{n \times n} \Sigma_{n \times d} V_{d \times d}^T \] where \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = r \leq \min(n, d)\).
Notice \((\sigma_{r+1}, \dots, \sigma_{\min(d,n)}) = 0\).

Truncated SVD (Skinny SVD):
Let - \(U_r := [u_1, \dots, u_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}\) - \(V_r := [v_1, \dots, v_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) - \(\Sigma_r = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)\)

then \[ X_{n \times d} = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T \]

11.5 Computing SVD

Power Method

Idea: Let \(C = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) and \(X = U \Sigma V^T = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T\). Then

\[ C = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^2 v_i v_i^T \quad\xrightarrow{\text{iterate}}\quad C^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^4 v_i v_i^T \quad\longrightarrow\quad C^k = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^{2k} v_i v_i^T . \]

If \(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2\), then for \(k\) large enough \(\sigma_1^{2k} \gg \sigma_2^{2k}\), so

\[ C^k \approx \sigma_1^{2k}\, v_1 v_1^T \;\Rightarrow\; C^k \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = C^k e_1 \approx \sigma_1^{2k}\, v_1(1)\, v_1 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{C^k e_1}{\|C^k e_1\|_2} = \pm\, v_1 . \]

Linear rate of convergence. To check the convergence rate, we look at

\[ \min\Big\{\, \|v^{(k)} - v_1\|_2^2,\ \|v^{(k)} + v_1\|_2^2 \,\Big\} = 2 - 2\,\big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle\big|, \qquad v^{(k)} := \frac{C^k e_1}{\|C^k e_1\|_2}. \]

Since the \(v_i\) are orthonormal,

\[ 1 = \|v^{(k)}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i} \big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_i\rangle\big|^2 = \big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle\big|^2 + \sum_{i=2}^{r} \big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_i\rangle\big|^2 . \]

For convergence we wish \(|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle| \approx 1\), i.e. the projection onto the other subspace is small:

\[ \frac{\sum_{i=2}^{r} \big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_i\rangle\big|^2}{\big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle\big|^2} = \frac{\sum_{i=2}^{r} \sigma_i^{4k}\, v_i^2(1)}{\sigma_1^{4k}\, v_1^2(1)} \le \frac{\sum_{i=2}^{r} \sigma_i^{4k}}{\sigma_1^{4k}\, v_1^2(1)} \le \frac{(r-1)\, \sigma_2^{4k}}{\sigma_1^{4k}\, \delta^2}, \qquad \text{if } |v_1(1)| \ge \delta > 0 , \]

hence

\[ \frac{1 - \big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle\big|^2}{\big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle\big|^2} \le (r-1)\, \frac{\sigma_2^{4k}}{\sigma_1^{4k}\, \delta^2} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{linear convergence, as } \sigma_2 < \sigma_1 . \]

Remark. Computing \(C^k\) is costly \(\to\) compute \(C^k q\) instead; each step only needs a matrix–vector product (efficient when one dimension of \(X\) is small, or \(X\) is sparse).

The same analysis holds for an arbitrary starting vector \(q\) (not just \(e_1\)):

Proposition 11.5 (Convergence to the near-top singular subspace) Let \(q \in \mathbb{R}^d\) with \(|\langle q, v_1\rangle| \ge \delta > 0\), and let \(U\) be the linear subspace spanned by the \(v_i\) such that \(\sigma_i \ge (1-\varepsilon)\sigma_1\). Let \(v^{(k)} = \dfrac{C^k q}{\|C^k q\|_2}\) be the \(k\)-th power iteration. Then it can be shown that

\[ \big\|\operatorname{Proj}_{U^{\perp}}\big(v^{(k)}\big)\big\|_2 \le \varepsilon, \qquad \forall\, \varepsilon > 0, \quad \text{when } k \ge \frac{\log(1/\varepsilon\delta)}{2\varepsilon}. \]

Proof. Expand \(q = \sum_{i} c_i v_i\) in the orthonormal basis \(\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_d\}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Then

\[ C^k q = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^{2k} v_i v_i^T \sum_{j} c_j v_j = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^{2k}\, c_i\, v_i, \qquad \|C^k q\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^{4k}\, c_i^2 . \]

For the first (single-vector) case, with \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots\),

\[ \frac{\sum_{i=2}^{r} \big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_i\rangle\big|^2}{\big|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1\rangle\big|^2} = \frac{\sum_{i=2}^{r} \sigma_i^{4k}\, c_i^2}{c_1^2\, \sigma_1^{4k}} \le \frac{\sum_{i=2}^{r} \sigma_i^{4k}}{c_1^2\, \sigma_1^{4k}} \le \Big(\tfrac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\Big)^{4k}\, (r-1)\, \frac{1}{\delta^2}, \]

where we assume \(\|q\|_2 = 1\) and \(|c_1| = |\langle v_1, q\rangle| \ge \delta > 0\).

For the subspace statement, suppose \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m\) satisfy \(\sigma_i \ge (1-\varepsilon)\sigma_1\), so that the component of \(v^{(k)}\) orthogonal to \(U\) is composed of indices \(i = m+1, \dots, r\). Then

\[ \big\|\operatorname{Proj}_{U^{\perp}}\big(v^{(k)}\big)\big\|_2^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=m+1}^{r} \sigma_i^{4k}\, c_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{r} c_i^2\, \sigma_i^{4k}} \le \frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{4k}\, \sigma_1^{4k}}{c_1^2\, \sigma_1^{4k}} \le \frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{4k}\, \sigma_1^{4k}}{\delta^2\, \sigma_1^{4k}}, \]

hence

\[ \big\|\operatorname{Proj}_{U^{\perp}}\big(v^{(k)}\big)\big\|_2 \le \frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}}{\delta} \le \frac{e^{-2k\varepsilon}}{\delta} \le \varepsilon \qquad \text{when } k \ge \frac{\ln(1/\varepsilon\delta)}{2\varepsilon}. \]

Remark. Note the above convergence analysis assumes \(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2\) strictly. In fact, the power method converges to some vector in the span of the singular vectors with “nearly highest” singular value.

\(q\) can be chosen at random, e.g. \(q \sim N(0, I_d)\). When chosen randomly, \(|\langle q, v_1\rangle| \ge \delta > 0\) can be shown to hold with high probability.

Subspace (Orthogonal Iteration) Power Method

Finding the right singular vectors is the same as finding the eigenvectors of \(X^T X\). One can use the orthogonal iteration algorithm for that.

Let \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) be positive semidefinite with eigendecomposition \(M = V \Lambda V^T = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \lambda_i v_i v_i^T\).

Orthogonal iteration

Initialize \(Q_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times \ell}\), a random \(\ell\)-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

Repeat until convergence:

  • \(t + 1 \leftarrow t\)
  • \(Z_{t+1} = M \cdot Q_t\)
  • \(Q_{t+1} = \mathrm{QR}[Z_{t+1}]\), where \(\mathrm{QR}[Z] := Q\) when \(Z = QR\) is the QR decomposition.

\(Q\) then represents the first \(\ell\) right singular vectors.

11.6 Best rank-\(k\) Approximation

Recap on matrix norms

Frobenius norm \(\|\cdot\|_F\): \[ \|X\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} x_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^2} \quad (\sigma_i \text{ singular values}). \]

Schatten \(p\)-norm \(\|\cdot\|_{S_p}\): operates on the singular values. For \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) with \(r \le \min(n,d)\) equal to \(\mathrm{rank}(X)\), \[ \|X\|_{S_p} := \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^p(X)\Big)^{1/p}, \qquad 1 \le p < \infty . \]

  • The spectral norm \(\|\cdot\|_2 = \|\cdot\|_{S_\infty} = \sigma_1\), and \(\|\cdot\|_F = \|\cdot\|_{S_2}\).
  • \(\displaystyle \|X - X_k\|_{S_p} = \min_{B:\ \mathrm{rank}(B) \le k} \|X - B\|_{S_p} = \Big(\sum_{i=k+1}^{r} \sigma_i^p\Big)^{1/p}\).

Best rank-\(k\) approximation

Definition 11.4 (\(k\)-truncated SVD) Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) with \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = r \le \min\{n, d\}\). The \(k\)-truncated SVD of \(X\) is \[ X_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T, \qquad k \in \{1, 2, \dots, r\}, \] where \(X = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T\) is the full SVD.

Theorem 11.2 (Best rank-\(k\) approximation (Eckart–Young–Mirsky)) \(X_k\) is the “best” rank-\(k\) approximation of \(X\) in terms of any orthogonally invariant norm \(\|\cdot\|\): \[ X_k = \underset{B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d},\ \mathrm{rank}(B) \le k}{\arg\min} \|X - B\| , \] i.e. \(X_k\) is the closest rank-\(k\) matrix to \(X\).

Remark. A norm \(\|\cdot\|\) is orthogonally invariant if \(\|O_1 X O_2\| = \|X\|\) for all \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) and all orthogonal \(O_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\), \(O_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\). Examples: the Frobenius norm \(\|\cdot\|_F\) and the spectral norm \(\|\cdot\|_2\).

Proof. Spectral norm. (In the manuscript \(A\) is used for \(X\) in this proof.) Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) with \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = r \le \min\{n,d\}\), \(X = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T\) and \(X_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T\). Then \[ \|X - X_k\|_2 = \Big\|\sum_{i=k+1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T\Big\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \] so we hope to show \(\|X - B\|_2 \ge \sigma_{k+1}\) for any \(B\) with \(\mathrm{rank}(B) \le k\).

Consider \(w \in \mathbb{R}^d\) with unit norm \(\|w\|_2 = 1\). We can choose \(w \in \mathrm{Null}(B)\), i.e. \(Bw = 0\); on the other hand, we can also choose \(w \in \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_{k+1}\}\). In this case \[ \|X w\|_2 = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 \cdots \sigma_{k+1}^2} \ge \sigma_{k+1} . \] Thus if \(w \in \mathrm{Null}(B) \cap \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_{k+1}\}\), then \[ \|X - B\|_2 = \max_{v \in \mathbb{R}^d,\, \|v\|=1} \|(X-B)v\|_2 \ge \|(X-B)w\|_2 = \|X w\|_2 \ge \sigma_{k+1} . \]

Proof. Frobenius norm. Consider \(\|X - B\|_F\), where \(\mathrm{rank}(B) = p \le k \le \mathrm{rank}(X) = r\). Denote \[ B = \begin{pmatrix} -\,b_1^T\,- \\ -\,b_2^T\,- \\ \vdots \\ -\,b_n^T\,- \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X = \begin{pmatrix} -\,x_1^T\,- \\ -\,x_2^T\,- \\ \vdots \\ -\,x_n^T\,- \end{pmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \|X - B\|_F^2 = \|x_1 - b_1\|_2^2 + \cdots + \|x_n - b_n\|_2^2 . \]

Let \(\{v_1, \dots, v_p\}\) be an orthonormal basis of the row subspace of \(B\), and extend to an orthonormal basis \(\{v_1, \dots, v_p, \dots, v_r\}\) for (the row space of) \(X\). Then expand \[ b_i = \beta_1^i v_1 + \beta_2^i v_2 + \cdots + \beta_p^i v_p, \qquad x_i = \chi_1^i v_1 + \chi_2^i v_2 + \cdots + \chi_r^i v_r , \] so \[ \|b_i - x_i\|_2^2 = (\beta_1^i - \chi_1^i)^2 + \cdots + (\beta_p^i - \chi_p^i)^2 + \sum_{l=p+1}^{r} (\chi_l^i)^2 , \] which is minimized when \(b_i\) is the projection of \(x_i\) onto the subspace, i.e. \(\beta_1^i = \chi_1^i,\ \beta_2^i = \chi_2^i,\ \dots,\ \beta_p^i = \chi_p^i\). (This can be visualized in the 3D case, where the distance between two vectors is minimized when \(b_i\) is the projection of \(x_i\) onto the plane.)

Since we are looking over all subspaces \(V\) of dimension at most \(k\), there could be a specific subspace that is better. As claimed previously, the greedy construction of singular vectors minimizes the distance between the vectors and the orthogonal subspace, so \(\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\) should be the first \(k\) singular vectors. The projection of \(X\) onto this subspace is \[ \hat{x}_j = (x_j^T v_1)v_1 + (x_j^T v_2)v_2 + \cdots + (x_j^T v_k)v_k , \qquad j = 1, \dots, n, \] which gives \[ \hat{X} = \begin{pmatrix} -\,\hat{x}_1^T\,- \\ \vdots \\ -\,\hat{x}_n^T\,- \end{pmatrix} = X \sum_{i=1}^{k} v_i v_i^T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T = X_k . \] Therefore \[ \|X - B\|_F \ge \|X - P_W^{p}(X)\|_F \ge \|X - P_W^{k}(X)\|_F \ge \|X - X_k\|_F . \]
