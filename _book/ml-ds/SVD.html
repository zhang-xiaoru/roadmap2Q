16  Singular Value Decomposition

16.1 Singular Vectors

Definition 16.1 (First right singular vector) The first right singular vector \(v_1\) of \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) is defined as: \(v_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d\), and \(v_1 = \text{argmax}_{\|v\|_2=1} \|Xv\|_2\) and the first singular value is \(\sigma_1 = \|Xv_1\|_2\)

Proposition 16.1 Definition 16.1 is equivalent as \[ v_1 = \underset{\|v\|_2=1}{\mathrm{argmin}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \|x_j - P_{v_1}(x_j)\|_2^2, \] where \(P_{v_1}(x_j)\) is the projected vector of \(x_j\) in the subspace spanned by \(v_1\). Here, we have \[ X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} = \begin{pmatrix} -x_1^T- \\ -x_2^T- \\ \vdots \\ -x_n^T- \end{pmatrix} \]

Proof. \[ \begin{align} \sum_{j} \|x_j - P_{v}(x_j)\|_2^2 &= \sum_{ij}\left(x_{i,j}-\langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_j\rangle v_i\right)^2 \\ &=\sum_{ij}x^2_{i, j} + \langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_j\rangle v_i^2 - 2x_{i,j}v_i\langle\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_j \rangle\\ &=\sum_{ij}x^2_{i,j} + \sum_j\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_j\rangle ^2 -2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_j\rangle^2\right)\\ &=C - \sum_j\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_j\rangle^2=C-\|X\mathbf{v}\|^2_2 \end{align} \] where \(C\) is a constant. Thus we have the \[ \underset{\|v\|_2=1}{\mathrm{argmin}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \|x_j - P_{v_1}(x_j)\|_2^2 = \underset{\|v\|_2=1}{\mathrm{argmax}} \|Xv\|_2^2 \]

Note

Proposition 16.1 shows that the first singular vector can be understood as a line that minimizes least square error of projected data, i.e., a best fitting line.

Next, we continue to define the second singular vector.

Definition 16.2 (Second Singular Vector) The second right singular vector \(v_2 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is defined as \[ v_2 = \underset{{v \perp v_1, \|v\|_2=1}}{\mathrm{argmin}} \|Xv\|_2, \] and second singular value defined correspondingly as \[ \sigma_2 = \|Xv_2\|_2 \]

Proposition 16.2 (Best-Fitting 2D Subspace) Let \(v_1, v_2\) be the first and second right singular vectors of \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\). Let \(V = \text{span}(v_1, v_2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) be the two-dimensional subspace they span.

Then \(V\) is the solution to the following minimization: \[ V = \underset{W:\,\dim(W)=2}{\mathrm{argmin}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \|x_j - P_W(x_j)\|_2^2 \] where \(P_W:\mathbb{R}^d\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2\) is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace \(W\).

Proof. Let \(w_1, w_2\) be two orthonormal bases for any 2-dimensional subspace \(W\). We can always select \(w_2\) s.t. \(\langle w_2,v_1 \rangle = 0\) (such \(w_2\) can be found by the intersection between \(W\) and subspace that perpendicular to \(v_1\)).

Definition 16.3 (r-right Singular Vector) The leading \(r\) right singular vectors \(v_1, \dots, v_r \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are defined as:

\[ v_k := \underset{ \substack{ \|v\|_2=1,\, \\ v \perp \text{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}\} } }{\mathrm{argmax}}{} \|Xv\|_2 \]

with the corresponding singular values as \(\sigma_k = \|Xv_k\|_2\).

Proposition 16.3 (Greedy construction for best-fit subspace) Let \(v_1, \dots, v_r\) be the leading right singular vectors constructed by Definition 16.3, and \(V = \mathrm{span}(v_1, \dots, v_r) \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) be the subspace spanned by them. Then \[ V \in \underset{ \substack{W\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d \\ \mathrm{dim}(W) = r \\ \text{linear subspace}} }{\mathrm{argmin}}\sum_j \|x_j - P_W(x_j)\|_2^2 \] i.e., \(V\) is the best-fit linear subspace in terms of least square error.

Remark 16.1.

  • Proposition 16.3 can be proved similarly as in the 2 dimensional case (Proposition 16.2).
  • Proposition 16.3 shows that the greedy construction of r-right singularvectors formed a best-fit subspace in terms of least square error.

16.2 Singular Value Decomposition

Theorem 16.1 (Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)) For any matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), there exist orthogonal matrices \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) and \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) and a non-negative diagonal matrix \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) such that

\[ X_{n \times d} = U_{n \times n}\, \Sigma_{n \times d}\, V_{d \times d}^T \]

Remark 16.2.

  • The diagonals of \(\Sigma\), arranged in non-increasing order, are the singular values defined in Proposition 16.3.
  • Columns of \(U\) are the left singular vectors of \(X\).
  • The left singular vectors \(u_i := \frac{1}{\sigma_i} X v_i\), where \(\sigma_i\) is the \(i\)-th diagonal element of \(\Sigma\).
  • Columns of \(V\) are the right singular vectors of \(X\) defined in Proposition 16.3.
  • SVD can be viewed as ‘generalized diagonolization’ of an arbitrary matrix.

The existence of SVD can be shown as follow

Proof 16.1. Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) and \(C = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), then \(C\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Let the orthogonal decomposition of \(C\) be: \[ C = V \Lambda V^T, \] where \(V^T V = I\) and \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_d), \lambda_i \geq 0 ,\forall i\in\{1, 2, \cdots, d\}\).

Consider \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) such that \(\Sigma^T \Sigma = \Lambda\). If \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = r \leq d\), we can arrange the diagonal element of \(\Lambda\) s.t. \(\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_r\geq0,\lambda_{r+1} = \cdots=\lambda_d=0\). Then \[ \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\cdots,\sqrt{\lambda_r}) & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

We define the left singular vector \(u_i\) as: \[ u_i := \frac{1}{\sigma_i} X v_i,\quad i = 1, \dots, r. \] Then \(u_i\) are orthonormal: \[ u_i^T u_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T X^T X v_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T C v_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \lambda_j \delta_{ij} = \delta_{ij}. \] We can then extend \(u_{r+1}, \dots, u_n\) such that \(U = [u_1\,|\,u_2\,|\,\dots\,|\,u_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is orthogonal.
Thus, we have \(\sigma_i u_i = X v_i\) for \(i=1,\ldots, r\) and \(X v_i = 0\) for \(i > r\), which gives \(X = U \Sigma V^T\).

Remark 16.3. This proof can be extended to \(X \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times d}\) with unitary matrices \(U\) and \(V\).

16.3 Greedy Construction and SVD

Proposition 16.4 The SVD of \(X = U \Sigma V^T\) is the same as the greedy construction; i.e., \(V\) and \(\Sigma\) are right singular vectors and singular values constructed by the greedy definitions.

Proof 16.2. Let \(v_1' := \mathrm{argmax}_{\|v\|_2=1} \|Xv\|_2\) be the first right singular vector.
Write \(X = U \Sigma V^T\) where \(V = [v_1, \dots, v_d] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) and \(v_i^T v_j = \delta_{ij}\).
Then: \[ \|Xv\|_2 = \|U \Sigma V^T v\|_2 = \|\Sigma V^T v\|_2 \] Let \(z = V^T v\), then \(\|z\|_2 = 1\) since \(V\) is orthogonal and \(\|v\|_2 = 1\).
So \[ \|Xv\|_2 = \|\Sigma z\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i} (\sigma_i z_i)^2} \] Suppose \(\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r\). Notice \[ \sum \sigma_i^2 z_i^2 \leq \sigma_1^2 \left(\sum z_i^2\right) = \sigma_1^2 \] Equality is attained when \(z_1 = 1\) \(\Rightarrow v = v_1 \Rightarrow v_1' = v_1\).

Next, \(v_2' := \mathrm{argmax}_{\|v\|_2=1,\, v \perp v_1} \|Xv\|_2\). We proceed similarly, but now \(z_1 = 0\) (since \(v \perp v_1\)).
Then \[ \|Xv\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=2} (\sigma_i z_i)^2} \leq \sigma_2 \] Equality when \(z_2 = 1 \Rightarrow v_2' = v_2\).
Similarly, we find all \(v_i\) by the greedy construction.

16.4 Skinny SVD and Full SVD

Full SVD:
\[ X_{n \times d} = U_{n \times n} \Sigma_{n \times d} V_{d \times d}^T \] where \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = r \leq \min(n, d)\).
Notice \((\sigma_{r+1}, \dots, \sigma_{\min(d,n)}) = 0\).

Truncated SVD (Skinny SVD):
Let - \(U_r := [u_1, \dots, u_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}\) - \(V_r := [v_1, \dots, v_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) - \(\Sigma_r = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)\)

then \[ X_{n \times d} = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T \]

If \(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2\), then for \(k\) large enough: \(\sigma_1^{2k} \gg \sigma_i^{2k} \Rightarrow C^k \approx \sigma_1^{2k} v_1 v_1^T\).Linear rate of convergence: To check the convergence rate, we check \(\min \{ \|v^{(k)} - v_1\|_2^2, \|v^{(k)} + v_1\|_2^2 \} = 2 - 2|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1 \rangle|\). \(v^{(k)} = \frac{C^k q}{\|C^k q\|_2}\) where \(q = \sum c_i v_i\). \(\frac{1 - |\langle v^{(k)}, v_1 \rangle|^2}{|\langle v^{(k)}, v_1 \rangle|^2} \le (d-1) \frac{\sigma_2^{4k}}{\sigma_1^{4k} c_1^2} \rightarrow\) linear convergence when \(\sigma_2 < \sigma_1\). Computing \(C^k\) is costly; instead, each step only needs matrix-vector multiplication (efficient when one dim of \(X\) is small or \(X\) is sparse).

Orthogonally invariant: \(\|O_1 X O_2\| [cite_start]= \|X\|\) for orthogonal \(O_1, O_2\). Example: Frobenius norm, Spectral norm.

Proof of Frobenius norm: Consider \(\|X - B\|_F^2\) where \(\text{rank}(B) = k \le r\). \(\|X - B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - b_i\|_2^2\). Let \(\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\) be the orthonormal basis for the subspace of \(B\). The error is minimized when \(b_i\) is the projection of \(x_i\) to the subspace. In previous claims, the greedy construction of singular vectors minimizes the distance between vectors and the orthogonal subspace. Thus \(\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\) should be the first \(k\) singular vectors. The projection of \(X\) is \(X_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T\).
