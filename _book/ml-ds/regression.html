20  Regression

Given a training set of predictor–response pairs, the goal of a regression problem is to learn a function \(f\) that predicts a (continuous) response from the predictors. This chapter develops linear and ridge regression, their solution via the normal equations, the extension to polynomial (linear-in-parameters) regression, and finally kernel regression together with the Woodbury identity that makes it computationally tractable.

20.1 Linear (Ridge) Regression

The general regression task

  • Let \(y_i \in \mathbb{R}\), \(i = 1, 2, \dots, N\) be the response variables.
  • Let \(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\), \(i = 1, 2, \dots, N\) be the predictors.
  • The pairs \(\{(x_i, y_i)\}\) form the training set.

The regression task wishes to find a function \(f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\) such that \(f(x_i) \approx y_i\), and that generalizes to the test set. This requires two ingredients:

  • a function form (parametric methods) — i.e. assume a family for \(f\);
  • a measure / metric for \(f(x_i) \approx y_i\) (the loss function).

Least squares (linear regression)

Take \(f\) to be linear: \[ y = w^{\mathsf T} x + b, \qquad w \in \mathbb{R}^d,\ b \in \mathbb{R}, \] or simply \(y = w^{\mathsf T} x\) if we absorb the bias by extending \[ x \ \longrightarrow\ \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_d \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} . \]

For the metric, use the least-square error \[ \min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^d} \ \sum_i \big(y_i - \langle w, x_i\rangle\big)^2 \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \text{error between predicted and actual,} \] or, in matrix form, \(\|Y - Xw\|_2^2\) if we denote \[ Y = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_N \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^N, \qquad X = \begin{pmatrix} x_1^{\mathsf T} \\ x_2^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ x_N^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d} . \]

Ridge regression (regularization)

In some cases \(f\) could overfit (e.g. matching noise). This can be reduced by adding a regularization term to the loss function, as a penalty for too-large coefficients: \[ \min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^d} \ \|Y - Xw\|_2^2 + \lambda \|w\|_2^2, \qquad \lambda \ge 0 . \]

From optimization theory, the resulting minimization problem is a strictly convex function (for \(\lambda > 0\)), so every local minimum is the global minimum.

Proposition 20.1 (Ridge regression solution (normal equations)) For \(\lambda > 0\), the ridge objective has the unique minimizer \[ w^\ast = \arg\min_w \ \|Xw - Y\|_2^2 + \lambda \|w\|_2^2 = \big(X^{\mathsf T} X + \lambda I\big)^{-1} X^{\mathsf T} y . \]

Proof. (Sketch.) Set the gradient to zero, \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} = 0\), and check the Hessian \(\dfrac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_i \partial w_j}\). This is straightforward since \(L\) is in quadratic form (and the \(\lambda I\) term makes \(X^{\mathsf T} X + \lambda I\) positive definite, hence invertible). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark. Choice of regularizer.

  • \(\lambda \|w\|_1 = \lambda \sum_i |w_i|\) — promotes a sparse weight vector.
  • \(\|w\|_\ast = \sum_i \sigma_i(w)\) (the nuclear norm) — promotes a low-rank matrix.

Remark. Multiple-label predictors. If \(y_i \in \mathbb{R}^p\) (multiple labels), we simply extend to a matrix of weights \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times d}\): \[ y_i = W x_i, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} y_1^i \\ \vdots \\ y_p^i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} & \cdots & w_{1d} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ w_{p1} & w_{p2} & \cdots & w_{pd} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1^i \\ \vdots \\ x_d^i \end{pmatrix} , \] and with \[ Y = \begin{pmatrix} y_1^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ y_N^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X = \begin{pmatrix} x_1^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ x_N^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad L = \|X W^{\mathsf T} - Y\|_F^2 + \lambda \|W\|_F^2 . \]

20.2 Polynomial Regression

\(f\) need not be linear, as long as it is linear in its parameters.

Example (3rd-order polynomial fit). \[ y = w_3^{\mathsf T} x^3 + w_2^{\mathsf T} x^2 + w_1^{\mathsf T} x = \big(\, w_3^{\mathsf T} \mid w_2^{\mathsf T} \mid w_1^{\mathsf T} \,\big) \begin{pmatrix} x^3 \\ x^2 \\ x \end{pmatrix} , \] where \(x^k\) denotes the monomial (elementwise-power) feature expansion. This shares the same form as linear regression, so the same solution applies.

20.3 Kernel Regression

The kernel trick can be applied to the features \(x\). Suppose \(\phi : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^p\) is a feature map that transforms the original data to \[ \big(\phi(x_1), y_1\big), \big(\phi(x_2), y_2\big), \dots, \big(\phi(x_n), y_n\big) \in \mathbb{R}^p \times \mathbb{R} . \] Then perform linear ridge regression in the transformed feature space: \[ \min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^p} \ \sum_i \big(w^{\mathsf T} \phi(x_i) - y_i\big)^2 + \lambda \|w\|_2^2 . \] From Proposition 20.1, the optimal solution is \[ w^\ast = \big(\Phi^{\mathsf T}\Phi + \lambda I_p\big)^{-1} \Phi^{\mathsf T} y, \qquad \Phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi(x_1)^{\mathsf T} \\ \phi(x_2)^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ \phi(x_N)^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} . \] Just as before, directly computing \(\phi\) and \(w^\ast\) is very costly when \(p\) is large. We want to apply the kernel trick, which relies on the following identity.

The Woodbury identity

Lemma 20.1 (Woodbury matrix identity) \[ \big(\Phi^{\mathsf T}\Phi + \lambda I_p\big)^{-1} \Phi^{\mathsf T} = \Phi^{\mathsf T} \big(\Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I_n\big)^{-1} . \]

Proof. Take the SVD \(\Phi = U \Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\) (so \(\Phi^{\mathsf T} = V \Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T}\), \(\Phi^{\mathsf T}\Phi = V \Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\), and \(\Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T} = U \Sigma \Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T}\)).

Left-hand side. Using \(U^{\mathsf T} U = I\) and \(I_p = V V^{\mathsf T}\), \[ \begin{aligned} \text{LHS} &= \big((V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T})(U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}) + \lambda I_p\big)^{-1} V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} \\ &= \big(V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma V^{\mathsf T} + \lambda V V^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} \\ &= \big(V(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma + \lambda I_p) V^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} \\ &= V(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma + \lambda I_p)^{-1} V^{\mathsf T} V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} = V(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma + \lambda I_p)^{-1}\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} . \end{aligned} \]

Right-hand side. Using \(I_n = U U^{\mathsf T}\), \[ \begin{aligned} \text{RHS} &= V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T}\big((U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T})(V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T}) + \lambda I_n\big)^{-1} \\ &= V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T}\big(U\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} + \lambda U U^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} \\ &= V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T}\big(U(\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I_n) U^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} \\ &= V\Sigma^{\mathsf T} U^{\mathsf T} U(\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I_n)^{-1} U^{\mathsf T} = V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}(\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I_n)^{-1} U^{\mathsf T} . \end{aligned} \]

A detailed calculation on \(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}(\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I_n)^{-1}\) and \((\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma + \lambda I_p)^{-1}\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\) shows that both equal the same \(p \times n\) “pseudo-diagonal” matrix with entries \(\dfrac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}\). Thus \(\text{LHS} = \text{RHS}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Kernel ridge regression prediction

Theorem 20.1 (Kernel ridge regression predictor) Using Lemma 20.1, the prediction at a new point \(x\) is \[ y = w^{\ast\mathsf T}\phi(x) = \phi(x)^{\mathsf T} \Phi^{\mathsf T}\big(\Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I_n\big)^{-1} y = k_x^{\mathsf T}\big(K + \lambda I_n\big)^{-1} y , \] where \(K_{ij} = \phi(x_i)^{\mathsf T}\phi(x_j) = K(x_i, x_j)\) is the kernel (Gram) matrix, and \(k_x = \Phi\phi(x)\) has entries \((k_x)_i = \phi(x_i)^{\mathsf T}\phi(x) = K(x_i, x)\).

Proof. Expanding the products directly, \[ \Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix} \phi(x_1)^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ \phi(x_n)^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} \big(\phi(x_1), \ \dots, \ \phi(x_n)\big) = \begin{pmatrix} \phi(x_1)^{\mathsf T}\phi(x_1) & \phi(x_1)^{\mathsf T}\phi(x_2) & \cdots \\ \vdots & \ddots & \\ \end{pmatrix} = \big(K(x_i, x_j)\big) = K , \] and \[ \Phi\phi(x) = \begin{pmatrix} \phi(x_1)^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ \phi(x_n)^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} \phi(x) = \begin{pmatrix} \phi(x_1)^{\mathsf T}\phi(x) \\ \vdots \\ \phi(x_n)^{\mathsf T}\phi(x) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} K(x_1, x) \\ \vdots \\ K(x_n, x) \end{pmatrix} = k_x . \qquad\blacksquare \]

Computational cost

Cost of kernel ridge regression

Using the kernel trick, the cost breaks down as:

  • Precompute \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) — cost \(O(n^2)\) kernel evaluations.
  • Precompute \((K + \lambda I_n)^{-1} y\) — cost \(O(n^3)\) flops and \(O(n)\) storage.
  • Compute \(k_x\) — cost \(O(n)\) kernel evaluations.
  • Compute \(k_x^{\mathsf T}(K + \lambda I_n)^{-1} y\) — cost \(O(n)\) flops.
